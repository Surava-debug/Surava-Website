Moderna Turned a Profit. So Why Is the Stock Lower?

Analysts expected a loss of more than a dollar per share. Moderna earned 38 cents. Eleven days later, the stock trades below where it was before the report.

Company Focus · 3 min read · 17 November 2026
Own market research
  1. 1Key points and conclusions by Surava Capital
  2. 2Written with AI assistance (Claude)
  3. 3Reviewed by Surava Capital

Moderna reported its third-quarter results on 6 November, and they beat expectations by a wide margin. Analysts had expected a loss of about 1.10 dollars per share on revenue of roughly 889 million dollars. That figure is the consensus: the average forecast of the analysts who cover the stock. Moderna instead earned 38 cents per share on revenue of 1.31 billion dollars. Net income was 162 million dollars, against a loss of 200 million dollars a year earlier. It was the company's first profitable quarter in years.

Where the profit came from

Most of the extra revenue came from COVID vaccine sales outside the US, delivered under long-term supply deals with governments such as the UK, Canada and Australia. Costs fell too: research and development spending dropped 27% to 583 million dollars as late-stage trials for infectious diseases wound down. Management raised its 2026 revenue growth target to 15% to 25%, up from “up to 10%”, and now expects to end the year with 8.1 to 8.3 billion dollars in cash.

The fine print

This was one quarter, not a turnaround. Over the first nine months of 2026, Moderna still lost 1.96 billion dollars. A large part of the quarter's sales had not been paid yet: receivables, money customers owe but haven't paid, stood at 1.37 billion dollars at the end of September. And the cost of making the vaccines rose 54%, faster than revenue.

Up first, then down

The stock first rose. It closed about 5% higher on results day and hit a 52-week high of 280.80 dollars on 9 November. Then came Analyst Day on 12 November, where Moderna presented its plans to investors. It said it will spend more on its cancer pipeline: cash costs come down this year but rise again from 2027, to around 4 billion dollars a year, and it gave no revenue target for 2027. The stock fell 7% that day, along with most of biotech: 74 of 79 comparable companies closed lower. On 17 November it traded around 209 dollars, about 25% below the peak.

Was it a short squeeze?

A short squeeze happens when many traders have bet on a falling price and are forced to buy their shares back at once, which pushes the price up further. The data does not point to one here. On 20 October, the data provider ORTEX estimated that only 9.8% of freely traded shares were sold short, with 2 days to cover: short sellers could have bought back all their shares in about two days of normal trading. The fee to borrow shares for shorting was 0.48% a year, which is low and shows that few traders wanted to short the stock. FINRA's mid-October count was similar, at about 32.6 million shares and under two days to cover. The real squeeze happened in August, after the first Phase 3 data. The rise after earnings looks like ordinary buying on a strong beat, and the fall like “sell the news” plus worries about costs from 2027.

Why one good quarter doesn't explain the price

For all of 2026, analysts expect about 2.4 billion dollars in sales and still a loss of about 7.37 dollars per share. A stock that has multiplied several times this year is not being priced on vaccine sales. It is priced on intismeran, the personalised melanoma vaccine Moderna develops with Merck. That is why the earnings jump faded so quickly.

Key takeaway

Moderna's quarter was genuinely good: a surprise profit, a higher sales forecast and lower spending. But the stock is not priced on quarterly earnings. Investors are betting on the cancer vaccine, and Analyst Day showed that this bet gets more expensive from 2027. The pullback looks like investors repricing those costs, not short sellers being squeezed.
